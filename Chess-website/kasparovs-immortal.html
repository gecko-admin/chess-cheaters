Before you play Kasparov’s Immortal

The puzzle starts on Garry Kasparov's twenty-fourth move against Veselin Topalov at Wijk aan Zee in 1999. The black king stands on a7 with a bishop immediately behind it on a8. White's king is on b1, within sight of the black pawns on the queenside. Both sides have reasons to hurry. Your job is to find the move that Kasparov used to begin his attack. The board asks for a single white move.

Start by picking out the white pieces that already influence the area around a7. The knight on a5 covers b7 and c6. The queen on f4 looks along the diagonal through e5 towards d6, where the black queen stands. White's rook on e1 has a clear file in front of it. Try to find a way for those pieces to cooperate before reading further. Looking for a route into the seventh rank is a useful starting point.

Two pawns and a blocked diagonal

Find the pair of opposing pawns on the d-file. The white pawn on d5 protects c6 and e6. It matters that White controls c6, since that square lies only a knight's leap from the black king and the king might soon need to cross it. On the square directly behind it sits a black pawn on d4. Black also has a pawn on c5 that could capture a white piece landing on d4.

Look along the diagonal from d4 to a7. It passes through c5 and b6. If the c5-pawn captures something on d4, c5 becomes empty. If a white queen subsequently captures that same pawn, the queen lands on d4 with an open route to a7. That is a remarkable transformation to obtain from two captures on consecutive diagonals. Work it through with your eye on the black king, then ask which rook White could afford to use as the bait.

Why the second rook matters

Kasparov took the black d4-pawn with his rook from d1. This is the move the puzzle asks you to find. In the game Topalov accepted by moving his c5-pawn to d4. White could now capture the pawn with the queen, but Kasparov first sent the other rook from e1 to e7 with check. Trace the seventh rank from e7 across d7, c7 and b7 towards the king on a7. Every square along that line is vacant.

It looks as if Black's queen on d6 can simply take this second rook. Imagine her doing so. White's queen takes on d4 with check along the diagonal you examined earlier. If the black king retreats to b8, White can check again from b6. The knight on a5 protects b7 and controls c6, ready to jump into the attack. Try drawing that small group of pieces from memory. Their coordination makes the second rook offer much more plausible.

Follow the king to the other side of the pawns

Topalov met the rook check by moving his king to b6. Kasparov then played the queen capture on d4 with check. A queen on d4 still attacks b6 through c5. The black king took the knight on a5, heading towards White's side of the board. After White pushed the b-pawn to b4 with check, the black king went to a4. Look at that square in the original diagram. It is a long way from where most players would start looking for tactics.

Notice the purpose of each check in this part of the game. The rook check reduces the king's freedom along the seventh rank. The queen check uses a diagonal cleared by the original sacrifice. The pawn check controls a5, obliging the king to move again. Saying which square a check takes away is a stronger test of your calculation than remembering that something dramatic happens to the king.

Try the decision from Topalov's side

Go back to the position immediately after White's first rook sacrifice. Instead of taking the rook, Black could consider moving the king to b6 immediately. That distinction matters. The game shows what happened when Topalov accepted the sacrifice. It takes a different calculation to decide what would happen against a defender who declined it. A check, a capture and a tempting offer place quite different demands on an opponent.

Finish by trying to name the role of each white rook before moving either one. The d1-rook removes the obstructing pawn and tempts its neighbour off c5. The e1-rook reaches the seventh rank with tempo. If you can see both jobs in the starting diagram, you have taken something useful from Kasparov's Immortal even without memorising the many moves that followed.
