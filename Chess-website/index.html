Choose the imbalance you want to explore

These chess challenges let you start with a question that an ordinary opening rarely answers. Can a mass of pawns keep advancing against two queens? How many knights can cross an open board before three queens pick them off? Each starting position gives both armies a particular strength and leaves something conspicuously missing. Pick a challenge where you can name the advantage on each side. After a few moves, check whether either side has managed to use it.

For a quick decision with immediate consequences, try 3 Pawns One Move from Promotion vs 1 Rook. White’s pawns stand on a7, b7 and c7. Black’s rook is already on h8, looking down the rank where all three want to promote. Think about which new piece the rook can capture and where the rook will land afterwards. Then compare that short race with 30 Pawns vs 2 Queens. There the first promotion takes longer to arrange, but each pawn lost from the front may have another waiting behind it.

Match your challenge to a chess skill

Choose 4 Rooks vs 2 Queens if you enjoy tracing straight lines. Look for pairs of rooks that can work along the same rank or file. Then give yourself the black pieces and look for diagonal attacks that none of the rooks can copy. In 8 Knights vs 3 Queens, the geometry changes completely. A line drawn across the board reveals the queens’ attacks but tells you very little about the knights. Count possible landing squares and check which of them are defended.

For a slower game of opening and closing lines, try 8 Bishops and 8 Pawns vs 8 Knights and 8 Pawns. Moving a pawn can release a bishop and simultaneously surrender a square to an enemy knight. Look at both effects before making the push. For a stripped-back version of that decision, try 1 Knight vs 8 Pawns. A knight blockading one pawn cannot be everywhere at once. Pick the pawn you most want to stop and see how much progress the other seven can make while you are busy.

Get more out of the lopsided positions

In 3 Queens vs a Full Army, White begins with open space and Black begins with many ways to defend a single square. Before sending a queen into the pawn line, identify Black’s cheapest recapture. In Beat the Bot with 30 Queens, reach has a different limitation. White owns so many queens that pieces in the rear cannot move through the front ranks. Move one of the leading queens and check which team-mate now has a useful line. Playing those two challenges consecutively makes the effect of crowding particularly clear.

These are custom boards, and the starting armies shown here do not have one king on each side. In these positions a king, where present, is a piece that can be captured. A side that runs out of legal moves loses. That detail matters when a queen gives what looks like an ordinary check. The opponent may be able to ignore the attack and take something elsewhere. Scan the whole board for that reply before counting on a forcing sequence.

Compare two games fairly

Play the same position once as each side at the same computer setting. During the first game, identify one square you struggled to defend. During the second game, see whether you can exploit that weakness from the other side. You may discover that the apparent breakthrough costs a queen, needs an extra turn, or leaves a pawn just enough time to promote. Switching sides turns an impression into a question you can answer on the board.

To test your own version, change one feature at a time. Remove a single knight from the starting line or move one advanced pawn back a rank, then replay as the same colour. Keep a short record of the first exchange and the first piece to reach the far side. A final score alone can conceal the interesting difference. If one small change delayed promotion by three moves or allowed a rook to escape along a newly opened file, you have learned something you can look for in a regular game as well.

If you enjoy setting a challenge for a friend, agree on the starting position and a smaller goal before either player moves. Saving a particular pawn, bringing the last knight across the centre, or taking the first queen can produce a close contest even when the eventual winner seems obvious.
