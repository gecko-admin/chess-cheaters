Build a position on the chess board

Use this chess board to revisit the moment when you had to choose between two moves. Start with a position you can describe in a sentence, such as a rook on an open file trying to stop a passed pawn. A small position makes it easier to see what each move changes. If you have the pieces from an actual game in front of you, work across the board one rank at a time. Check whose turn it is before you start playing.

Open Start from and choose the standard board if most of the original pieces are still in play. For a sparse ending, choose the empty board and drag pieces across from the palette. To remove a piece, take hold of the piece itself and drop it off the board in setup. The coordinates around the edge help when copying a diagram. A white rook on a1 remains on a1 when you flip the board, even though you now see it in the opposite corner.

Try a position with three pieces

Set a white king on c6, a white pawn on c7 and a black king on a7. Leave White to move and choose the standard rules. Before playing, work out what would happen if the c-pawn became a queen. From c8 a queen controls b8 along its rank and b7 along its diagonal. The white king on c6 covers b6 and b7. Now look at the black king’s other neighbouring squares. Does it still have a move after promotion?

The answer is no. The queen’s diagonal continues through b7 to a6, and its rank also covers a8. The black king has no move and is not in check, so the game ends in stalemate. Try promoting to a rook instead and watch what happens to a6. You can use the same method with a position of your own. Select one candidate move, list the opponent’s replies and choose a reply to test against the computer.

Bring in a game with FEN

If another chess board gives you a FEN, choose Import FEN from Start from and paste the position. FEN records piece placement as a series of ranks, starting on Black’s back rank. It also carries information you cannot see from the pieces alone. Two diagrams can look identical while having different legal moves because one side can still castle or can capture a pawn en passant on that turn.

For instance, a white king on e1 and a rook on h1 may have returned to their starting squares after moving earlier. In an ordinary game those pieces have already lost the right to castle together. Counting the empty squares between them will never reveal that history. When reproducing a real middlegame, importing the original FEN preserves these details. After importing, compare the corner squares and the side to move with the source before analysing the position.

Test your move against the computer

There are two separate choices at the start of play. First move tells the board which colour acts next. Play computer lets you choose which army you control. Say the diagram has Black to move and you want to practise defending as White. Set the first move to Black, choose White when starting the game and let the computer make the opening move. Keeping those choices straight matters as much in a race between pawns as it does in a checkmating attack.

Pick a computer level that lets you work on the part of the position that interests you. If the question is whether a rook sacrifice works, write down the opponent’s strongest reply before using Find best move. Compare the suggested move with yours, then play out the reply you expected. One game tells you how a particular continuation unfolded. A second attempt with the same starting pieces lets you change a single decision and see its effect.

Keep the position you want to revisit

During a game, use Copy resume link at the point you want to keep. Give that link a descriptive name, especially if you intend to test two branches of the same position. A note such as “after Black takes the rook” will save you time when returning to the game. Use Undo to revisit a recent decision or Reset to play again from the starting position. For a different sort of exercise, open a challenge with uneven armies or choose a historical puzzle with a specific objective and a side already set to move.
