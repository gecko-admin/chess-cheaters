Choose a chess puzzle by its question

These ten chess puzzles ask several different questions. In The Opera Game you have to deliver mate in two white moves. In The Réti Study the aim is to save a draw with a king and a pawn. The Game of the Century asks for one decision by Black. Read the goal before touching a piece. A move that wins a queen can miss a mate in two, and a move that trades every pawn can be exactly right when a draw is the task.

For a first visit, choose The Opera Game and The Réti Study as a pair. The first position rewards a close look at Black’s back rank and the pieces that can reach it. The second rewards counting the king’s steps towards two distant pawns. Give each position the same few minutes of attention. Notice how differently you use that time when you know that forcing checks might solve one problem and that the other has no rook, bishop or queen on the board.

Work backwards from checkmate

In a short mating puzzle, start by naming the squares next to the enemy king. Which squares are occupied by its own pieces? Which are empty but attacked? Those answers tell you what a final checking move would have to cover. Next, inspect the checks available now. If one looks promising, try to find an escape for the defender. Count a capture of the checker, a block on the checking line and a king move as separate possibilities.

The Immortal Game gives you a three-move mating task. Before playing the first move, try describing the final position you hope to create, even if you cannot yet name every square. The Evergreen Game gives the attacker several pieces with long lines. On that board it pays to follow a bishop’s diagonal all the way to its end whenever another piece moves. A line closed in the diagram can become the decisive feature after a single reply.

Read an endgame one tempo at a time

The Réti Study and The Saavedra Position both feature a pawn that wants to promote, yet the defender’s resources differ. In Réti, count the white king’s journey twice. First calculate how long it takes to approach the black pawn. Then count how long it takes to support its own. On an eight-by-eight board a diagonal step can make progress towards two targets at once. Look for the turn when the opponent’s choice finally commits you to one of them.

Saavedra adds a rook to the calculation. A pawn on the seventh rank may look a single move from victory while a checking rook still has several ways to gain time. Each time the rook checks, identify the king squares that keep the pawn’s prospects alive. When promotion comes into view, compare the legal pieces and check the defender’s reply to each. This habit matters in practical endings whenever the seemingly automatic queen might leave the defender a surprising resource.

Look beyond the first spectacular move

Marshall’s Gold Coins Game, The Game of the Century and Kasparov’s Immortal each ask you to find a particular move from a historical game. A correct first move completes the challenge on the page. To get more from it, pick the most tempting opposing capture and calculate what the attacker plays next. Then examine a reply that declines the offer. This gives you two concrete reasons for your decision and helps you recognise when a similar sacrifice would fail.

The Windmill offers a different test. Follow the checking piece and the line it uncovers after every move. Before accepting a free piece, ask whether you can return with another check. In Excelsior, try the original challenge of guessing the mating piece before you move. Keep your prediction to yourself until the puzzle finishes. If it proves wrong, replay the early moves and look for the first clue that your chosen piece was unlikely to give mate.

Return to the positions you nearly solved

On a second pass, take a puzzle you recognised but could not finish. Before moving, say what you missed in ordinary language. Perhaps the king could capture the checking piece, a rook could interpose or a pawn could promote with check. Look specifically for that resource throughout the line. If remembering the first move feels too easy, pause after the reply and try to explain the next move without relying on the picture you remember.

Use each puzzle’s own page when keeping a shortlist. A list with one mating attack, one endgame and one first-move challenge makes for a compact practice session. Record the title and the question that caught you out. When you return, let that question guide your calculation before asking for a hint.
