Open a diagonal in 8 Bishops and 8 Pawns vs 8 Knights and 8 Pawns

White begins with eight bishops on the first rank and eight pawns immediately in front of them. Black has the same pawn screen and a knight on every square of the eighth rank. Pick up the white bishop on d1 in your imagination. Its first steps would be c2 and e2, but White occupies both squares. Every bishop in the formation has the same problem. White’s first move must be a pawn move, and the choice decides which bishops can join the game.

Try d2 to d4. The bishop on c1 now has a route through d2 and e3 towards f4. The bishop on e1 can pass through d2 towards c3, b4 and a5. One central pawn move gives two bishops different jobs. Compare that with a2 to a4. Vacating a2 gives the bishop on b1 a new square, but the diagonal ends at the edge there. Both pawns travel the same distance. Their effect on the rest of the army differs considerably.

See the opening through Black’s eyes

Black can begin developing while leaving every pawn in place. The knight on b8 can jump to a6 or c6 over its own pawn screen. On c6 it attacks d4, so it can challenge White’s advanced d-pawn straight away. Before capturing that pawn on a later turn, check for a white pawn on c3 or e3. Either could recapture on d4, and a retreating knight cannot undo a trade it has already made.

Look at a different starting knight as well. The one on a8 can reach b6 immediately, while c7 is occupied by a friendly pawn. Compare the destinations by asking what each knight can attack next. A knight that has crossed the pawn line may still have to retreat because every forward jump is guarded. Developing another knight can provide a partner for the first or occupy its only safe exit. Work out which before committing another piece to the same patch of board.

Count bishops by square colour

White starts with four light-squared bishops and four dark-squared bishops. A bishop that begins on c1 always stays on the same colour as c1. Even seven surviving bishops cannot compensate for losing every bishop that travels on one colour when the target sits on that colour. For a practical exercise, select an advanced black knight and trace diagonals backwards from its square. Which white bishops could ever capture it, and which could capture it on the very next turn?

Bishops on the same colour can work on different diagonals. They can also get in each other’s way. If one bishop sits between a second bishop and a knight, only the nearer bishop attacks the knight directly. Move it off the diagonal and the second bishop may acquire a threat the opponent has overlooked. Keep the farther bishop in mind when deciding whether the first can afford to leave its post.

Decide which pawns should meet

Suppose White has a pawn on d4 and Black advances a pawn to d5. Those two pawns block each other. They still attack squares on either side, which can help a knight settle near the centre. Look for a white pawn that could challenge the pawn on d5 with a capture from c4 or e4. Opening that file has little meaning for an army without rooks. Opening the adjoining diagonals can change what several bishops can do at once.

For Black, a useful outpost is a square where a knight has a target and White has difficulty chasing it away with a pawn. Count any bishops that could trade for the knight there. If White exchanges and a black pawn recaptures, examine the new pawn structure before calling the exchange a success. The recapture might close the diagonal that White wanted, or it might move the only pawn that was holding another knight in place.

Compare two opening experiments

Play once with a central pawn push and record how many bishops have a clear diagonal after White’s third turn. Start again with a wing pawn and count again. Next take Black and see whether you can put two knights in positions where they defend each other while attacking different white pawns. These small assignments make this unusually crowded board easier to read. The opening question is which of your eight pieces the next pawn move will release, support or strand. Track a single bishop through both games. If it still sits at home, identify the particular pawn in its way and decide whether moving that pawn would expose anything behind it.
