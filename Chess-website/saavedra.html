Four pieces and a winning pawn

The Saavedra Position begins with White's king on b6, a white pawn on c6, Black's king on a1 and a black rook on d5. White has the move. The puzzle comes from analysis associated with Georges Barbier and Fernando Saavedra in 1895. Its demand sounds ambitious for the side with a lone pawn. White has to win against a rook whose checks can reach across the board. The position of the distant black king will matter just as much as the race to promote.

Before playing, find the promotion square and inspect the four squares around the black king in the corner, including the one it occupies. Imagine a white piece suddenly appearing on c8. Which of those squares would a queen cover? How would that change if the new piece were a rook? Keep your answer in mind, then concentrate on the immediate problem of escaping the checks. The promotion decision can wait until you have earned it.

Walk the king down the board

White plays 1.c7. If given a quiet turn, the pawn will promote on c8. Black answers 1...Rd6+, attacking the king along the sixth rank. The white king steps to b5. Black follows with 2...Rd5+, and White goes to b4. After 3...Rd4+ the king continues to b3. Notice that the white pawn has spent this entire sequence on the seventh rank. Its threat forces Black to find moves that deal with it while occupying White's attention.

After 4...Rd3+ the white king changes direction with 5.Kc2. It now attacks the rook on d3. Draw the king's path from b6 to c2 using your finger. At each intermediate point Black's rook could check from the same rank. The final step changes the relationship between the two pieces. The king has come close enough to interfere with another rook check, while the pawn remains poised to promote on the opposite side of the board.

Inspect Black's trap

Black's 5...Rd4 looks like permission to take a queen. A useful way to solve the next part is to play the obvious promotion in your head and then give yourself Black's pieces. After 6.c8=Q, Black has 6...Rc4+! The rook checks the king along the c-file and places itself in reach of the new queen. If White takes with 7.Qxc4, examine the black king on a1 before declaring victory.

The queen on c4 guards a2 along the diagonal through b3. The white king on c2 guards b1 and b2. Black has lost the rook and has no legal king move. The king on a1 is not in check, so the result is stalemate. If White moves the king away from the check instead, the black rook has the queen on its file. Black's trap works because the queen does two jobs on c4 when White would prefer it did only one.

Choose the piece that leaves a flight square

White's winning choice in the puzzle is 6.c8=R. A rook promoted on c8 still controls the c-file. If Black now tried the same 6...Rc4+ and White captured on c4, a2 would be free for the black king. A white rook cannot attack that square along the diagonal from c4. White would have a normal winning rook ending against a bare king. One missing diagonal changes the value of the promotion completely.

Once White has a rook on c8, Ra8 would deliver a dangerous check down the a-file. White's king on c2 already guards both b1 and b2, leaving Black's king no route onto the b-file. Black plays 6...Ra4 to prevent that invasion. White then plays 7.Kb3, attacking the rook on a4 and threatening Rc1 mate. Follow how the direction of attack changes when the white king takes one step to the left and forwards.

Work backwards from the final threat

Look closely at why the move to b3 matters. The white king now covers a2. The rook on c8 can travel down the clear c-file to c1. From there it would attack a1 along the first rank, and the king on b3 also bars b2. Black must address that threat with its rook under attack. Try shifting the rook up the a-file in your mind and check whether the white king still covers the escape square.

Reset and compare the two hypothetical captures on c4 side by side. Name the square the queen controls and the rook leaves free. That single square explains the underpromotion more clearly than a rule about choosing weaker pieces. In another ending, run the same stalemate check before accepting a rook offered with check.
