An evening at the opera

Paul Morphy played the Opera Game in Paris in 1858 against the Duke of Brunswick and Count Isouard, who consulted together as Black. You join them at White's penultimate move. White has a queen on b3, a bishop on g5 and a rook on d1. Black still has a queen, rook, bishop and knight. Count the pieces and Black seems to have every reason to continue. Look at which pieces can reach the king in one move and a different picture emerges.

Your assignment is mate in two. That means White must give checkmate on the second white move even if Black chooses the strongest legal defence. Before moving anything, pick the square on the eighth rank where you would most like to place the rook. Then look for the piece standing between the rook and its destination. Understanding that obstruction makes Morphy's finish much easier to reconstruct from memory.

Read the three lines of attack

Start at g5 and trace the bishop's diagonal through f6 and e7 to d8. Now start at d1 and travel up the d-file. Both white pieces point towards d8, although the black knight on d7 blocks the rook's path. Finally, follow the queen up the b-file. All the squares between b3 and b8 are empty. You can do this exercise without calculating a single capture. It shows which white pieces already have the reach to affect the back rank.

Black's bishop on f8 takes away a square from its own king. On the other side, the queen on e6 sits between the king and some of the activity in the centre. Neither piece can easily interrupt a check delivered at very close quarters. See whether you can find a first check which leaves Black with a defence but makes that defence perform a job for White.

The solution to the Opera Game

Morphy played 16.Qb8+. The queen travels five squares along the open b-file and checks horizontally. The knight on d7 can capture on b8. It is worth pausing before allowing that capture on the board. The queen seems to have placed herself on a square the knight can take. Yet the moment the knight lands there, the d-file becomes clear all the way from the white rook to the black back rank.

After 16...Nxb8, White plays 17.Rd8 mate. The rook attacks the king from the adjacent square. A piece cannot interpose when there is no square between attacker and king. The king cannot take on d8 because the bishop on g5 protects it. The rook also covers e8 and the bishop covers e7. Look at the black pieces on b8, e6 and h8 in this final picture. Each would be a welcome defender if it could reach the rook in time.

Try two different first moves

Reset the position and investigate 16.Rxd7. On the surface, capturing the knight looks like a convenient way to open the file. The white rook itself now stands on d7, though, and the move gives no check. Black has a turn to intervene. Compare this with enticing the knight away using a check. Morphy needed both an open line and the opportunity to use it immediately. Winning the piece blocking the line was a different undertaking.

Now consider sending the queen to b7. It is a tempting square near the king, and it even puts the queen next to the knight's eventual destination. On b7 the queen does not check the king on e8. Identifying the exact line along which a check travels is a good safeguard against seeing a beautiful finish one move earlier than it really works. Say the intervening squares aloud if you lose the line in your head.

Remember the bishop that stayed still

Replay the diagram once from Black's side. List the legal ways to answer the opening check. Try moving the king, blocking the line and taking the attacker. When you arrive at the rook check, repeat the same three tests. This time there is no answer. Comparing those two moments explains the queen sacrifice more precisely than simply awarding it an exclamation mark.

On your next attempt, keep your attention on g5 while moving the queen and rook. White's bishop never moves during the solution, yet without its protection Black could take the checking rook with the king. That quiet contribution is the detail to carry into another game. When two of your pieces share a target, check whether a forcing move by a third can clear the route between them.
