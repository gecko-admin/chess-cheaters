Read the Réti Study guide (includes solution)

A pawn race starting in the corner

The Réti Study puts four pieces on the board and asks White to draw. Richard Réti's 1921 position begins with the white king on h8, its pawn on c6, the black king on a6 and a black pawn on h5. White moves first. Across the board, Black's king is already close to the white pawn. Along the h-file, Black's pawn is several steps ahead of White's king. At first glance both races seem to favour Black.

Try predicting the result of each race on its own. If both kings disappeared, which pawn would reach the last rank first with White to move? If the c-pawn disappeared instead, could White's king run straight down the h-file and catch the other pawn? The answers point in opposite directions. Restoring all four pieces creates a choice for Black about where to spend time. White's solution keeps that choice open long enough to save the game.

Count king moves on a diagonal

A king can change file and rank in the same move. Count the moves from h8 to h5, then from h8 to e5. Both journeys take three moves on an empty board. The second route has carried the king three files closer to White's own pawn at no extra cost in ranks. That simple measurement explains why a route through the centre deserves a closer look in this endgame.

The tempting direct chase 1.Kh7 leaves the white king on the h-file. Once Black pushes the pawn, White cannot catch it by following directly behind. Meanwhile the king has done little to help the c-pawn get through. Before you choose your first move in the puzzle, find a square from which the king could turn towards either wing. Then ask what Black would have to do to make one of those destinations irrelevant.

Follow the line to two queens

White begins 1.Kg7. In the line on this board, Black answers 1...Kb6, approaching the white pawn. After 2.Kf6 h4, White plays 3.Ke5. Look at the board before playing Black's next move in your head. If the black king now takes on c6, it spends a turn that its h-pawn needs. White can move towards f4 and catch the advancing pawn. That possibility is what makes the central route so valuable.

Here Black continues 3...h3 instead. White turns towards its own pawn with 4.Kd6. Following 4...h2, White plays 5.c7. Black promotes first with 5...h1=Q, but the new queen gives no check against a king on d6. White has time for 6.c8=Q. Look at the two new queens, the kings and the empty files where the pawns used to stand. This is the drawn ending that the puzzle line reaches.

Why both branches matter

Return to the position after the white king reaches e5. Imagine you are Black and could remove the pawn on c6 without using a turn. You could then advance your h-pawn immediately. In a real game the capture takes a whole move. White uses precisely that time to approach the h-pawn. It pays to count turns afresh at each branch rather than assuming that the faster-looking pawn will always keep its original lead.

Now choose the pawn push instead. Black can queen quickly, yet leaves the c-pawn standing. The white king's move to d6 supports c7 against capture by the black king on b6. Notice how the value of d6 depends on the presence of the pawn. A calculation that considers only the distance from White's king to h1 will completely miss the reason to go there. Recalculate once as the hunter of the h-pawn and once as the escort of the c-pawn.

A practical test for your own endgames

On a fresh attempt, cover the move list and pause after each king move. Name one square you would head towards if Black pushed the h-pawn and another you would head towards if Black moved the king. As the solution progresses those answers change. By the time the white king goes to d6 it has committed itself to promotion, and you should be able to explain which black decision made that commitment sound.

When analysing another pawn race, write down whose move it will be immediately after the first promotion. Check whether the new queen gives check, captures the opposing pawn or prevents its promotion from a distance. Every one of those details can change the outcome. In the Réti Study the white king ends far from its starting corner, its own pawn survives, and Black's first queen arrives without gaining the extra turn it needs.
