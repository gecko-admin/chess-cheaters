Before you play Excelsior

Sam Loyd gives you two puzzles in one. White must mate in five moves, and you get a second guess for free before touching the board. Which white piece will give the last check? Pick one and name the square from which you think it will do the job. The extra question is worth answering. Getting the move sequence right and finding a convincing picture of the finish are quite different achievements here.

The black king is on h1. His own pieces are scattered across the board and neither army has a queen. White has two rooks, the sort of pieces you would gladly choose for a job on the back rank. Five moves also sounds like a generous allowance for bringing one of them into play. Try the puzzle now if you would like to make your first guess unaided. The next section gives a positional clue.

What the corner tells you

Count the squares beside h1. The white rook on e2 already controls g2 and h2 along the second rank. The same rook does nothing to stop the king stepping sideways to g1. A rook arriving on the first rank could supply that missing piece of the net. Look at the white rook on b5 and find two files it could use to descend. Then ask what Black might do while it changes files.

There is a trap in imagining that only the black king matters. White's king stands on h5, level with the rook on b5. Move that rook along the fifth rank in your head and look down from Black's rook on c8. A black rook appearing on c5 could pin the white rook against its king. White needs a way to meet interference on precisely that square. Finding a piece that can protect c5 is a better clue than counting checks.

Following the pawn after your attempt

The key is b4. The pawn on b2 uses its double step, putting c5 under attack. In the line on this board Black still plays the rook to c5, this time with check, and White captures it with the pawn. Notice where the other white rook stands as you make the capture. The rook on b5 suddenly has a clear path down the b-file and threatens to reach b1 with mate.

Black's advanced a-pawn steps to a2. If a rook now lands on b1, the pawn can take it there and promote. White changes plans and pushes the c5-pawn to c6. Moving the pawn off the fifth rank also clears the way for the rook on b5 to travel across it again. On a board with so few pieces, a single pawn has managed both to obstruct its own rook and to release it one move later.

Reading the last diagonal

After the bishop goes to c7, White's pawn captures on b7. Its next capture will land on a8, where Black's knight has been sitting since the diagram position. Trace the diagonal from a8 through b7, c6, d5, e4, f3 and g2 to the king on h1. Two of those squares were occupied by the travelling pawn itself a moment ago. Its captures have arranged for the whole diagonal to be empty at exactly the right time.

The board follows the queen promotion. A bishop would give the same diagonal checkmate in the composition. Cover the rank and file through a8 with your fingers and check which escape squares remain available to the king. That little experiment explains why the bishop has all the power the job requires. It also gives you a way to explain the underpromotion without relying on what an engine says.

Reconstruct the five white turns

For a final challenge, start again and remember only the pawn's destinations. They are b4, c5, c6, b7 and a8. On a second pass, say what changes for the b5-rook each time the pawn moves. You should be able to explain the first three turns without mentioning promotion at all. That is a good test of whether you understand why Black cannot simply spend five leisurely moves watching the pawn advance.

Excelsior gives its name to the problem theme in which a pawn travels from its starting square to promotion during the solution. What gives Loyd's example its character is how long the rooks remain credible candidates for the finish. If you chose a rook before playing, look again at the diagram. You spotted a real mating idea. That very threat is what gives the unlikely pawn enough time to cross the board.
