Before playing The Evergreen Game

You join Adolf Anderssen and Jean Dufresne on White's twentieth move. Look at g2 before examining the tempting checks against the black king. Black's queen is on f3 and a rook looks down the g-file. White's king on g1 sits directly behind the g2-pawn. It is an uncomfortable place to spend a move tidying up the position. White needs to make threats that demand attention immediately.

There is plenty of attacking talent on White's side too. The rooks sit together on d1 and e1, the bishops occupy a3 and d3, and the queen is out on a4. Most of these pieces stand some distance from the target on e8. Which ones could attack the black king after someone else moved out of their way? Have an attempt on the board before continuing. The next clue looks at the lines of attack without giving the first move.

The files through the middle

Run a finger from the rook on d1 up the d-file. It bumps straight into a friendly bishop on d3. Turn the exercise around and follow the bishop's diagonal from d3 through e4 to f5. A bishop moving to f5 could give a check of its own against a king on d7, while simultaneously uncovering the rook's attack up the d-file. That king is currently on e8. The question is how to persuade it to change squares.

Now look at the other white bishop on a3. It looks through b4, c5 and d6 to e7. While you calculate, treat the bishops separately. One works with the rook on d1 and the other can capture something on e7. It is surprisingly easy to muddle their jobs once pieces start coming off the board. The two bishops occupy opposite colours, so imagining the colour of a destination square can help you keep track.

Why the queen goes to d7

In the game Anderssen began with the e1-rook, capturing the knight on e7 with check. Dufresne answered by bringing his other knight from c6 to e7 to take the rook. That reply clears c6, a detail worth pausing to admire. White's queen on a4 now has a diagonal path through b5 and c6 to the pawn on d7. The piece that captured White's rook has opened the route for White's queen.

White takes the d7-pawn with check. After the king captures the queen, stop and count how much heavy material White has given up. Then ignore the tally and make the bishop move you investigated earlier. From f5 the bishop checks along e6 to d7, and the rook on d1 checks the same king along the newly cleared file. Capturing one attacker or blocking one line cannot answer both checks. Black has to move the king.

Where the two bishops finish

In the continuation on this page the king returns to e8. The bishop on f5 comes back along e6 to d7 and gives check. Notice how the surviving rook protects d7 all the way from the first rank. This time the king heads for f8. Before playing the finish, find the black knight that captured the first white rook. It has remained on its new square ever since.

The other white bishop takes that knight on e7 and gives mate. The bishops now stand side by side on d7 and e7. To audit the finish, identify what happens on e8, what protects the bishop on e7 and what prevents a flight to g8. White's d7-bishop attacks e8, the f6-pawn protects e7, and Black's own rook occupies g8. Pieces of both colours help to enclose the king, even though only one bishop delivers the final check.

Replay it from Black's seat

The Evergreen Game puzzle follows the moves Dufresne actually played. On the first check Black could also consider moving the king to d8. That gives a different calculation, so memorising the final arrangement of the bishops will only answer part of the question. For a useful second attempt, stop after each white check and name every possible king move before looking at the reply chosen for you.

Then try to reconstruct the game finish from the three squares c6, d3 and e7. Remember what vacated the first square, which line opened through the second and who eventually arrived on the third. Those three details recover most of the combination. They are also easier to recognise in your own games than a remembered string of moves from a position you might never see again.
