Read the shape of the pawn front

In 30 Pawns vs 2 Queens, the sheer number of white pawns can obscure the most important detail. Only a few can advance. White fills ranks two, three and four completely, then adds six pawns on the fifth rank from b5 through g5. There is no pawn on a5 or h5. Black has queens on d8 and e8. With White to move, the first decision belongs to the front of the formation. The rear two ranks must wait for the pawns ahead to give them space.

Pick one file and name every pawn in order. On the c-file they stand on c2, c3, c4 and c5. If c5 advances, c4 can follow on a later turn. The pawn on c3 must wait longer still. On the a-file there are only three, so a4 can already step to a5. Counting these queues explains why thirty pawns cannot immediately overwhelm two defenders. One side has a huge reserve of pieces, while the other can change its point of attack in a single move.

Check the landing square after every push

Try c5 to c6. At c5 the pawn had support from b4 and d4. On c6 it has support from b5 and d5 instead. The destination looks safer when you can name those defenders. Now compare the move b5 to b6. The pawn on c5 protects b6, but a5 is empty. A pawn near the edge can have fewer neighbours even when the board is packed. Before advancing two neighbouring pawns in succession, recalculate who will still be guarding each one.

A defended pawn can still be taken. The interesting question is what the recapture changes. If a white pawn captures towards the centre, it vacates its old file and joins another queue. A second black queen may be able to use the newly opened file to reach the rear. As White, look at the board after the recapture you intend to make. Is your next pawn ready to advance, or have you created one more obstacle to its promotion?

Give the queens different assignments

Play Black for a round and choose one queen to guard a promotion route. Give the other queen freedom to attack. Two queens hovering over the same advanced pawn often duplicate effort. Once the fast pawn is safely contained, the second queen can look behind the leading line for a pawn whose removal would isolate its neighbour. Trace any proposed capture all the way back to the queen’s current square. A pawn standing between the two squares blocks even a perfectly aimed queen.

The forward edge of the formation and the last rank present different problems. On the fifth rank a pawn still needs time to promote. On the seventh it can transform on its very next move if its path is clear. From Black’s side, pay particular attention to a pair of pawns that could arrive on different promotion squares. Count whether a single queen can cover both squares after White’s next advance. If she must capture one pawn, check whether her new square still covers the other.

One simple defensive drill is to ignore captures for a moment and identify every safe square from which a queen could blockade the leading pawn. Then ask which neighbouring pawn could attack that square after just one push. A blockade that survives this test may buy enough time for the second queen to return.

Play through the first promotion

White can practise by naming a candidate for promotion on move one. Keep track of its original file even if it captures across to a different one. When it finally reaches the last rank, pause before choosing the new piece. A queen supplies the long-range movement the pawn army has lacked throughout the game. Occasionally a knight promotion attacks a target that a new queen would miss. Look for the actual knight jump and ask what the black queens can capture immediately afterwards.

This board has no kings, so there is no check to give and no king to escort the pawns. The result depends on the moves the two armies have left. A surviving pawn stranded in front of a blocker may contribute little to that result. After your first game, reset and choose a different pawn on the opening turn. Record the first promotion, the first lost queen and the file where Black broke through. Comparing those three events gives a more revealing answer to “who wins?” than counting the pawns that happened to survive one game.
