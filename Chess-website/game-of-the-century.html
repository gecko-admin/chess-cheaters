Take Fischer's seat on move seventeen

The Game of the Century pairs Donald Byrne with thirteen-year-old Bobby Fischer in New York in 1956. You play Black at the moment when Byrne's bishop on c5 attacks Fischer's queen on b6. The white king is on f1, its rook on h1 still tucked into the corner. Across the centre, Black has a knight on c3 and a rook on e8. A glance at the queen suggests an urgent retreat. Give yourself a minute to inspect the king on f1 before deciding how urgent the retreat really is.

Unlike a mate-in-two problem, this puzzle asks for a particular move from a game. You only need to find Black's seventeenth move on the board. To understand it, though, you will want to follow what happens if White takes the queen. Try to predict which black piece can make the first check in that continuation. Knowing the answer will help you judge whether leaving a queen under attack is a calculated decision.

Locate the pieces that can join with tempo

The black bishop on g4 has an open route back along the diagonal through f5 to e6. From e6 it would look through d5 towards the white bishop on c4. Trace that second diagonal past c4 as far as it goes. Once a black bishop lands there it can see d3, e2 and f1 in succession. The white king sits at the far end of that line. Keep the bishop on c4 distinct from the other white bishop which threatens the queen from c5.

Black's knight on c3 can jump to e2. From e2 a knight attacks a king on g1. It also temporarily stands across the bishop's route from c4 to f1. That sounds like an inconvenience until you imagine the knight leaving e2 again. The bishop would immediately regain its attack on f1. This is the mechanism behind a discovered check, where moving one piece uncovers a check from another. The piece that moves can capture something valuable at the same time.

The move that offers the queen

Fischer played 17...Be6. This quiet bishop move gives no immediate check and leaves the queen en prise on b6. Its threat becomes clear after Byrne's 18.Bxb6. White's bishop travels from c5 to b6 and takes the queen. Black replies 18...Bxc4+, taking White's other bishop and checking along the diagonal to f1. Notice the order. Had White's king been elsewhere, capturing a bishop might have allowed White time to consolidate a material advantage.

In the game White played 19.Kg1, and Fischer continued 19...Ne2+. After 20.Kf1 the knight captured on d4 with check. The knight on d4 does not itself attack f1. Look back to the bishop on c4, which can see the king again now that e2 is empty. That distinction is worth checking on a board because the knight's new square draws the eye away from the piece actually giving the check.

Follow the knight as it collects material

The white king returned to g1, whereupon the knight could check from e2 once again. After the king returned to f1, Black played ...Nc3+, uncovering the bishop's check for a second time. The knight has travelled through a square it already visited, but a pawn has disappeared from d4 in the meantime. Look at the material and piece locations before calling any repeated sequence a draw. Returning a king to its former square is only part of the picture.

The bishop which captured the queen on b6 is also exposed. Black's a7-pawn can capture it on b6 once the time is right. The checks allow Fischer to dictate the order of events while Byrne has to answer threats to his king. When counting the return for the queen, include the pieces Black can actually capture during the forcing sequence. Adding up everything under attack at the beginning would count imaginary gains as readily as real ones.

Practise the calculation in reverse

Start again and imagine that you have only been told that Black wants a bishop on c4 with check. Find a route there from its original square on g4. Which intermediate square prepares the capture? Which white piece has the option of taking the black queen in the meantime? Those two questions lead directly back to the move the puzzle expects, without relying on the name of the game or Fischer's reputation for brilliant tactics.

For a final test, explain the position to someone using the two white bishops as your landmarks. One wins the queen and ends up a target. The other occupies the entry square for Black's checking bishop. When both roles are clear, you can look for a similar opportunity in your own games by tracing the diagonal from the opposing king backwards to a possible attacker.
