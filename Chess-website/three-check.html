What earns a check in Three-Check?

Three-Check starts with a familiar board and two separate tallies. White wins by giving Black three checks, and Black wins by giving White three. Checkmate can finish the game even when the winning side has given fewer than three. A check still has to be legal. If a move exposes your own king, an attack on the far king cannot rescue it. The status beside the game shows both totals, so read it before deciding what kind of threat you face.

One move adds one to your tally when the resulting position checks the enemy king. A discovered check counts, as does a checking capture or a checking promotion. A double check is still a single checking move. Another piece may deliver your next check, and quiet moves can separate the first check from the second. Losing the piece that gave a check does not subtract the point. That last detail changes the arithmetic of a sacrifice.

When is a checking sacrifice worthwhile?

Imagine your opponent has two checks and you have two. A legal check finishes the game immediately, even if the checking piece could have been captured on the following turn. Search all your pieces before worrying about their price. A knight jumping onto a defended square, a pawn taking beside the king or a bishop sliding just one square can all finish. Trace the complete attack from the destination square to the king to confirm that an intervening piece does not block it.

Now change your tally to zero. The identical sacrifice may simply leave the opponent a piece ahead after the reply. Put the likely capturing piece on its new square in your imagination. Is there another check available there? Does accepting the sacrifice open a file towards the king? If the reply closes every route, think about developing another attacker first. When reviewing a game, note the count at the time of the decision. Remembering only the position leaves out an essential part of the problem.

Build a sequence with two rooks

Clear the board and place White’s king on a1 and rooks on a2 and b1. Put Black’s king on h8 and give White the move. Start with Ra8+. Look at the seventh rank before moving Black’s king in your head. It can step to g7 or h7. From either square, White can swing the b1 rook up to b7 with another check. Neither white rook has had to approach the king closely. They are taking turns cutting across open ranks.

Follow one continuation precisely. After Ra8+, Kh7 and Rb7+, the black king can go to h6. Can you see the third check without moving the b7 rook? The rook on a8 can descend to a6, attacking along the sixth rank. Reset and vary Black’s first reply. Name the square the black king actually reached before you look for another check. This keeps the exercise about finding lines on a changing board rather than memorising three white rook moves.

Defend when the count is against you

Against someone with two checks, protecting a checking square by attacking it may achieve very little. A protected square can still host the final check. Start by identifying where a checker could land legally. A knight’s jumps cannot be blocked in flight, so taking the knight, moving the king or preventing it from reaching a checking square may be urgent. Against a rook, a single well-placed blocker can interrupt attacks from several squares along the same file.

Before playing a tempting counterattack, give your opponent an imaginary turn after it. List every check, beginning with pawns and knights because their attacks are easy to overlook when a queen is near the king. If one survives, your counterattack needs to finish the game now or remove that check. A threat of mate on your next turn offers little protection if the opponent can give check number three immediately.

Keep a scorecard for one game

For one practice game, write down each check and the reply it forced. Afterwards, mark the moments when the opponent had several safe replies and the moments when just one square or capture worked. Revisit the least effective check. Try a quiet move that brings a second piece into the attack, then see whether the original check becomes more forcing. You will learn which checks helped build the third one and which merely helped the opposing king reach shelter. Then switch colours and try defending against the same attacking idea.
