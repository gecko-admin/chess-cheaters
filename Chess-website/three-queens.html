Look through the pawn screen

In 3 Queens vs a Full Army, White starts with queens on c1, d1 and e1. Black has the familiar starting army, right down to the eight pawns along the seventh rank. White has the first move and almost every file is open. Before choosing a capture, trace the three queens’ lines up the board. Each can see a pawn directly ahead. Now trace the line beyond each pawn. The piece waiting on the eighth rank often decides whether that tempting capture is worth making.

Take the c-file as an example. The white queen on c1 can capture the pawn on c7 straight away. The queen on d8 then has a diagonal capture on c7. White’s other two queens cannot recapture the black queen there from their starting squares. That single reply changes an attractive raid into the loss of a third of White’s army. Make the same inspection on the d-file and the e-file. Seeing an undefended stretch of board is quite different from seeing a safe landing square.

Give each queen a separate job

Try describing a job for each queen before you move. One might control the only good retreat square of a black piece. A second might support the square where the third intends to capture. With only three pieces available, those jobs frequently overlap. A move that puts two queens on the same useful diagonal can also uncover a file you were previously guarding. Follow both changes whenever you shift a queen from one side of the board to the other.

A quiet example is moving the queen from c1 to c3. From there she looks along the diagonal through d4, e5 and f6 towards g7. She also looks back towards e1 through d2. The queen on e1, in turn, protects c3 along that same diagonal. Play the move and see which lines the computer opens in response. If Black plays a pawn to f6, for instance, that pawn now interrupts the route to g7. Redraw the diagonal from the new position before continuing the original plan.

Play the army from the other side

Black’s first difficulty is finding room. The bishops begin behind their own pawns and the rooks begin behind the corner pawns. A knight can jump out without waiting, but its destination matters. If White has posted a queen on c3, a knight arriving on f6 enters her diagonal. Check what protects f6 and whether trading pieces there would open the other queen’s route into Black’s camp. Developing a knight has consequences for the pieces that stay behind.

Choose one pawn move whose purpose you can name. Moving the e-pawn opens the diagonal from f8 through e7. Moving the d-pawn opens the diagonal from c8 through d7. Work out where that bishop could go on a later move, then look at what White could take immediately after the pawn advances. An opening that makes sense in ordinary chess can hand several white queens access to the same file here. That tension gives Black a concrete reason to delay some natural-looking moves.

Count the replies after a trade

White has no king in this setup. The app therefore plays this as a custom material challenge. Black’s king can be captured, and capturing it alone does not end the game while Black still has a legal move. An attack on the king cannot force a reply by the ordinary rules of check. Before playing what looks like a queen fork against king and rook, ask whether Black can simply move the rook, strike at your queen or make an even larger capture elsewhere.

For a second attempt, pick a single early exchange and change only the decision immediately before it. Play once with the attacking queen supported and once with her operating alone. Compare what remains after Black’s best recapture, including which side gets the next move. White might prefer a retreat that preserves three active queens, while Black might willingly lose several pawns to remove one. A useful verdict names the square where that difference appeared. It gives you something concrete to look for when the next three-queen attack reaches the edge of the pawn screen.

For a final test, play Black and count how many of your original back-rank pieces have moved by the time the first white queen comes off. If most are still at home, identify the defender that actually did the work. Its position may be worth preserving in your next game even when development elsewhere looks more urgent.
