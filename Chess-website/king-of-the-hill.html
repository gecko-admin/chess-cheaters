Find the four winning squares

In King of the Hill there are four summits. A legal king move to d4, e4, d5 or e5 wins immediately. You can also win by checkmate. The four central squares on the board are marked so you can see the objective even when pieces crowd the middle. A king standing on c4 is one move away from a possible finish. A king on a1 needs several moves, and each move gives the opponent a turn to change the route.

Begin with two kings, White on c3 and Black on h8. White can play Kd4 and finish in a single move. Move Black’s king to e5 before starting and the picture changes completely. Black already occupies a winning square. For a playable defensive exercise put Black on e6 instead. That king controls d5 and e5 but does not attack d4. White can still win straight away. Counting which central squares are controlled is more informative than saying that Black is close to the middle.

Count moves along legal routes

Look at the shortest possible route for each king, then test whether it is open. A white king on e1 could reach e4 in three straight steps on an empty board. In the initial position, its own pawn occupies e2. Once pieces move, a black bishop or a rook on the e-file might make another step illegal. A route down the adjacent file can be faster in practice if the path you counted first requires two preparatory moves.

When you compare two routes, write the squares down. Suppose your king can approach through c3 or e3. Look a move beyond each arrival. From c3 the only summit within a king move is d4. From e3 both d4 and e4 are candidates. The second route gives the defender two entries to cover, although one black piece might still cover both. List the defender’s best reply before you commit. A route is only as quick as its slowest necessary detour.

Give a supporting piece a specific job

A white pawn on c4 attacks d5, while one on f4 attacks e5. Those small facts matter when Black tries to enter from the sixth rank. A bishop can cover two summits on a diagonal. On g2, a white bishop with a clear diagonal controls e4 and d5. Put a white pawn on f3 and both controls disappear at once. If you move a pawn to make room for your king, look behind it for a newly opened enemy line as well.

A knight offers coverage from unexpected angles. From f6 a black knight attacks e4 and d5. Imagine that your king on e3 was planning Ke4 and that your bishop can exchange itself for the knight. Work out who recaptures and what that piece covers from f6. Removing the first defender only helps when the replacement leaves a legal entry. This is a good place to use the setup board, trying the same position with a pawn and then a bishop ready to recapture.

Practise defending the last step

Give White a king on c3 and a rook on a1. Give Black a king on h8 and a rook on d8. Leave the d-file empty and give White the move. The immediate Kd4 is illegal because of the black rook. Which white move would you try to improve access on the following turn? Consider moving the king towards e3, or using the white rook to challenge Black’s control. Let the computer respond and see whether its move opens another approach.

Play the same position as Black. Before checking the white king, decide which square your rook will still cover from its destination. A check that drives the king closer to e4 and abandons the d-file can be a poor bargain. Sometimes simply keeping the file closed leaves the opponent with a longer route than yours. Compare the kings again whenever a rook changes file or a diagonal opens.

Recognise the moment to finish

At the start of every turn, inspect all four marked squares. If your king can enter safely, the result is immediate. You do not need to survive a reply or keep control for another move. If you have no legal entry, inspect the opponent’s next step before choosing a capture. In a review, find the last turn when both sides were still two moves away. That is often where an overlooked pawn move, clearance or unguarded diagonal first decided the race.
