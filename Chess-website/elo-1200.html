Build a shortlist before calculating

Play 1200 Elo chess to practise choosing between plausible moves. One develops a rook, another wins a pawn, and a third attacks the queen. Each has a purpose. Deciding which purpose matters now is the exercise. The number marks an approximate computer difficulty and the opponent can still miss a tactic. A useful session goal is to explain your choice at three turning points, including one position where neither player has an immediate threat.

Begin by describing the last move in two parts. Which square did the opposing piece reach, and which square did it leave? A queen retreat can open a file for a rook. A knight move can uncover a bishop’s diagonal. Once you have accounted for those changes, choose two candidates and look for the strongest reply to each. You can often discard one quickly because it allows a check or abandons a defender. Spend your calculation time on the more difficult choice.

Test a pin with real squares

Set up a white king on g1, a white bishop on g5, a black king on e7 and a black knight on f6. White’s bishop, the knight and the king lie on one diagonal. Give Black the move and inspect the knight. Moving it away would expose its king to the bishop, so those attractive knight jumps are unavailable. Black could move the king off the diagonal if the destination is safe. That single move changes how you must count the knight’s attacks on the next turn.

Now replace the black king on e7 with a black queen and put the king on a8. The line still matters, but a knight move can now be legal. Whether moving the knight is sensible depends on what that move achieves and whether the queen falls afterwards. In your next game, name the piece behind a pinned defender. That detail helps you distinguish an impossible defence from an expensive defence the computer might nevertheless choose.

Learn what an exchange does to the pawns

Imagine a common decision in which your bishop can take a knight on c6 and Black can reply with a pawn from b7 or d7. Look beyond the value of the minor pieces. A b-pawn recapture leaves the b-file free of that black pawn and places another pawn nearer the centre. A d-pawn recapture clears d7 and may open a diagonal for the bishop on c8. Which recapture Black prefers depends on the rest of the position. A doubled pawn can come with active pieces and useful open lines.

Take this habit into your games with a simple question before each voluntary exchange. Which piece becomes easier to develop after the dust settles? If the answer is an enemy bishop that previously had no useful move, ask whether you can maintain the tension instead. A small improvement to your own position may be worth more than resolving an exchange immediately.

Give the rooks an entry square

An open file can look impressive while leading nowhere. Before doubling rooks, find a square on that file where one rook could actually enter. Is it defended by a pawn? Can the opposing rook trade there? If you could reach the seventh rank, which pawn or king would become a target? These questions give a quiet move a destination and a reason. Sometimes the useful move is one that removes the piece guarding the entry square.

During a computer game, pause when the queens come off and locate each side’s most active rook. Predict the next file it would like. If yours is trapped behind a fixed pawn, consider which route lets it participate soonest. If the opponent already controls the only open file, check whether contesting it forces a favourable exchange or just walks into a tactic on the back rank.

Review the first fork in your plan

Pick a decision where your two candidates led to genuinely different positions. Return to it and play the move you passed over. Compare practical details after the reply by checking king safety, loose pieces, pawn targets and the ease of developing the remaining forces. If the computer chooses a different defence, include that defence in your review. The point of a second attempt is to discover what your first calculation omitted. Take one question from that review into your next 1200 Elo game and use it before you touch a piece. A particularly useful comparison is whether you still like both candidates when you imagine facing them from the other side.
