Map the first set of jumps

In 8 Knights vs 3 Queens, White’s eight knights start shoulder to shoulder from a1 to h1. Black’s queens occupy c8, d8 and e8. With six empty ranks between the armies, the difference in reach is visible before anyone moves. The queens can cross the board in one turn. The knights have to find a sequence of landing squares. White’s first task is to build a route where a knight can survive long enough to make its second jump.

Start with the knight on d1. It can reach b2, c3, e3 or f2. A jump to c3 puts it on the open c-file in front of the queen on c8. The knight from b1 also guards c3, so a capture there would allow a recapture. Compare that with a jump from d1 to b2. The king’s usual starting square e1 currently holds a knight, but that knight cannot guard b2 from e1. Name the actual defender of each destination before relying on the impression that eight knights must cover everything.

Repeat this inspection from the edge. The knight on a1 has only b3 and c2 available. Which of those landings do other starting knights guard? Once you can answer from White’s side, turn the board around and see how much easier those isolated squares are for Black to spot.

Count the colours between two squares

Knights change square colour with every jump. This gives you a fast way to reject an imaginary route. Suppose a knight needs to reach a particular square in two moves. Its destination must be the same colour as its starting square. Passing that test does not guarantee a route, so identify the intermediate landing square too. Against three queens, that intermediate square is often the difficult one. A perfectly good geometric route can take two turns through a square a queen already controls.

The colour rule also applies to forks. All squares a knight attacks from a single post have the same colour. The queens on c8 and e8 begin on matching colours, and a white knight on d6 would attack both at once. The queen on d8 complicates that dream because she can look straight down the d-file towards d6. Before planning several jumps towards the fork, see whether Black can capture the knight from the third queen’s square. A target diagram is only useful when you include every defender.

Build pairs with a purpose

A pair of knights can guard each other, but moving either member breaks that connection. Try keeping one knight near the centre as an anchor while the other advances. Before the second knight moves, name the square that will lose protection. Then look for a back-rank knight that can replace the missing defender on its next turn. A chain built this way can travel further than a group that keeps chasing the nearest queen, even when the individual moves look less aggressive.

From Black’s side, examine the outermost knights first. A knight near an edge has fewer landing squares. A queen may be able to cover several of those squares from a safe distance, leaving another queen free to attack the knight itself. Move a queen in your imagination and then inspect her new square for knight attacks from every direction. A queen can lose to a backward jump just as easily as a forward jump. The white pieces have no reason to approach along a straight line.

Test whether a fork earns anything

When a knight attacks two queens, work through three questions. Can a queen take the knight immediately? Can a different queen move with an attack on another valuable white piece? If White captures one queen next turn, can Black remove two knights during the exchange? Write down the final board you expect after the dust settles. This challenge has no kings and no checks, so a fork cannot compel a response merely by including a royal target. The material and the surviving moves tell you what the fork achieved.

For another attempt, play the queens and keep a tally of white knights that reach the fifth rank. If several get there together, trace their earlier jumps and find the first landing square that went uncontested. If none reaches it, switch sides and try spreading the knights across both wings before approaching the centre. A useful improvement might be as small as reaching the same fork square with a second knight guarding it. That single difference can turn a queen’s easy capture into a trade she would much prefer to avoid.
