Before playing Marshall’s Gold Coins Game

Play the black pieces in the position Frank Marshall reached against Stepan Levitsky in 1912. The task takes just one move on the board. The satisfying part takes rather longer. Once you have picked a square for Black's queen, you want an answer ready for each white piece that could capture her there. Try naming those pieces before giving yourself credit for finding Marshall's idea.

White's heavy pieces are busy. The rook on c5 attacks the queen on c3 along the c-file. The queen on g5 is close to the black king, while the white rook on f1 stays beside its own king. Across the board Black has a rook on h3, a knight on d4 and another rook on f8. If you are solving unaided, pause here. The next section examines a checking square that appears in several different continuations.

A knight check with three consequences

From d4, Black's knight can jump to e2 and give check to a king on g1. Imagine the knight already on e2. It would attack g1 and g3 at the same time. A white queen arriving on g3 would therefore land within reach of a knight fork. White might tolerate the fork if a pawn could recapture the knight afterwards. Look closely at what moving the f2-pawn would expose.

The black rook on f8 already faces the white rook on f1 along the f-file. White's f2-pawn interrupts the line. At the other edge of the board, the black rook on h3 looks towards h1 through a white pawn on h2. Both white pawns near the king seem to offer protection. Each also plugs a line that leads directly back into White's position. Ask what falls open if either pawn leaves its post.

The queen offer and the two pawn captures

Marshall played his queen from c3 to g3. The queen threatens to take h2 with mate, backed up by the rook on h3. Two pawns and a queen can capture the intruder. Start with the h-pawn. After hxg3 the knight jumps to e2 and gives mate at once. The white king cannot escape to h1. By leaving h2, its own pawn has opened the entire h-file below the black rook.

Try the f-pawn instead. After fxg3 the knight again jumps to e2 with check. This time the pawn on h2 remains in place, so the black rook on h3 cannot cover h1 and White can move the king there. But the pawn that used to plug the f-file has gone. Black's other rook comes down from f8, takes the rook on f1 and mates along the first rank. The first check was identical. The finish changed because a different pawn took the queen.

What if the white queen captures?

White's queen can travel from g5 through g4 to capture on g3. Both pawns stay home, which makes this defence more resilient. Follow the knight. It jumps to e2 with check, forcing the king away, then captures the queen on g3 with another check against the king on h1. White could take the knight with the f-pawn, but by now you know what happens to the rook on f1 when that pawn moves.

Returning the king to g1 keeps White alive for longer. Black can then take the f1-rook with the knight. White may capture the h3-rook in return, but Black can save the knight and emerge a piece ahead. This branch is an excellent excuse to keep calculating after the spectacular part of a combination. A queen sacrifice may end with an extra knight and ordinary work still to do in an endgame.

A three-square memory test

Levitsky resigned after the queen landed on g3. The captures you have analysed are possible replies, so you will not find them as further moves played in the game. To check your understanding, start over and try describing the winning move without saying that the queen is immune. It very much can be captured. What matters is being able to specify the price White pays for each choice.

Remember three squares for your next game. Look at h1 to test the h-pawn capture, f1 to test the f-pawn capture and g3 to see the knight fork after the queen capture. With those landmarks in mind, the most unusual feature of Marshall's move becomes much easier to spot. It offers three captures on exactly the square where all three of Black's supporting pieces have something to say.
