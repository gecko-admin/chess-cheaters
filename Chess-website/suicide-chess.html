Who is doing the capturing?

The quickest introduction to Suicide Chess takes two pieces. Put a white rook on a1, a black rook on h8 and give White the move. Slide the white rook to a8. Black has a capture along the eighth rank and must take it. White comes to move with an empty board and wins. Now reset and play Rh1 instead. The same finish happens along a file. Ordinary rook geometry has supplied two winning moves, once you know whose pieces you want to disappear.

Captures are compulsory for both players. When two captures are legal, the player moving chooses which to make. Without a capture, any otherwise legal move is available. A player who has no legal move wins, even with pieces left on the board. Kings can be captured and have no special protection. A rook can sit next to the enemy king, a king can walk into an attacked square and a player can ignore an attack on their own king. There is no castling here.

Take away the choice

A good offer leaves a reply you can predict. Before offering a bishop to a pawn, check whether that pawn can capture something on its other diagonal. Then inspect every other enemy piece. A rook on the far edge can spoil a perfectly good idea by taking a different piece. Count the opponent’s choices after your intended move. If there are three, you have three lines to consider, however attractive your favourite one looks.

Try a small extension of the rook exercise. Keep the original two rooks and add a white pawn on h7. White plays Ra8. This time the black rook on h8 can take either the rook on a8 or the pawn on h7. Play each reply on your board. Where does the surviving white piece stand, and does it have a compulsory capture of its own? Changing one square has turned an immediate finish into a calculation about the next turn.

Plan the landing square

A piece that accepts an offer ends up somewhere. Use that destination to arrange a second offer. A long diagonal can allow a bishop to eat a succession of pieces, provided the next offering stays on the bishop’s colour. A knight has a different appetite. Mark its landing square, then count its possible jumps from there. The square it attacked before capturing can tell you very little about what it will attack afterwards.

Pawns are particularly good for practising this way of thinking. A black pawn on d5 can take a white piece on c4 or e4. If it takes on c4, a white piece on b3 may be the next meal. White still has a turn between those captures. Check whether White can keep the b3 piece in place or has a compulsory capture that breaks up the sequence. Every link in a forced line includes a move by both sides.

Aim to run out of moves

Sometimes leaving a pawn on the board is exactly what you want. Give White a lone pawn on a2, Black a lone pawn on a3 and set White to move. White cannot advance into an occupied square and has nothing to take on b3. That is a win for White under these rules. Move the black pawn across to b3 and try again. White now has to capture, leaving Black to move without a piece. The same two pawns produce opposite results.

The exercise explains why a pawn near the edge can be easier to immobilise. It has only one diagonal to inspect. It also explains why casually taking the opponent’s final piece can lose immediately. Before a capture late in the game, stop on the imagined resulting board and ask which player gets the next turn. Counting survivors without identifying that player misses the result.

Review the move before the mistake

After a game, find the first turn where you were forced to take something you wanted to leave alone. Go back one move further. Could you have placed your piece on a different colour, kept a file closed or offered something that changed the capturing piece’s route? Play from that earlier position against the computer, keeping the same side. A good review ends with a specific observation you can test again. For example, the bishop only had a choice because the second diagonal opened after your pawn advanced. Try leaving the pawn where it stood and play the line again.
